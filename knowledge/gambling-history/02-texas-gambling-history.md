# Gambling in Texas: From Frontier Cards to the State Lottery

*Neutral history and cultural reference. Describes the evolution of gambling in Texas and the
figures who shaped it. Not a how-to for any illegal operation.*

Texas has one of the more contradictory gambling histories in the country: some of the strictest
anti-gambling laws in the nation, alongside decades of tolerated vice and larger-than-life
operators. The arc runs from open frontier saloons, through a long reform-and-underground era, to
the voter-approved **Texas Lottery** of the early 1990s.

## The frontier era (1800s–early 1900s)

In the rough days of the Texas frontier, gambling was as common as cattle drives. Saloons in San
Antonio, Houston, and Dallas featured poker, dice, and informal betting on everything from horse
races to boxing matches. Cowboys driving cattle north stopped in Fort Worth, Dallas, San Antonio,
and Houston, where saloons offered days-long poker games, **faro** tables run by professional
dealers, dice, and high-stakes games involving cattle, land, and oil rights.

Two economic booms poured fuel on this culture. **Railroad expansion** in the late 1800s sent
gambling houses up in railroad towns, with professional gamblers following construction crews. And
the **discovery of oil at Spindletop in 1901** brought a flood of workers and speculators;
boomtowns like Beaumont saw gambling halls operate openly, and successful wildcatters often parlayed
fortunes into gambling establishments. Oil speculation was itself a kind of gambling, and the two
cultures intertwined.

## Reform and the underground (early 1900s–1930s)

As Texas society settled, religious groups and reform politicians — with significant **Baptist**
influence in state politics — pushed for anti-gambling laws. But enforcement was often selective:
well-connected operators flourished while smaller competitors were prosecuted. Gambling simply moved
into underground networks — private clubs, back-room poker, and informal betting circles throughout
Texas cities.

## Mid-century contradictions (1940s–1980s)

Texas maintained contradictory positions. Casino-style gambling stayed illegal, yet the famous
**"Chicken Ranch"** brothel near La Grange operated for decades with unofficial tolerance —
emblematic of the gap between the law on the books and practice on the ground. Meanwhile the state
permitted some legal gambling: **horse racing** with pari-mutuel betting, **church bingo**, and
**charity raffles**. Many Texans simply traveled — to Louisiana riverboat casinos or across the
border into Mexico — creating a regional gaming-tourism economy.

## The numbers game in Texas

Texas cities ran their own versions of the **numbers game** (see the numbers-running article),
serving growing African-American and Mexican-American communities. Local businesses — barbershops,
restaurants, corner stores — served as collection points, creating an informal economic network
that provided employment and credit. San Antonio's cultural mix produced a distinctive scene that
blended Anglo card games, Mexican **lotería** traditions, and African-American numbers games; the
city's military presence added demand near bases. As elsewhere, players built folk systems from
oil-well production numbers, cattle-auction totals, and cotton prices.

## Famous Texas gambling figures

### Benny Binion (1904–1989)

Lester Ben "Benny" Binion, born in rural Grayson County north of Dallas, became Texas's greatest
gambling figure and later a Las Vegas pioneer. His father was a gambler, and Binion was exposed to
the world early. His start was running a lucrative **numbers operation** in Texas, which he grew
into some of the most successful in the Southwest, alongside high-stakes poker for oil men and
cattle barons, sports betting, and protection rackets. In 1931 he was convicted of killing a
rival; the violent, competitive nature of Prohibition-era Texas gambling is part of his record. In
**1951** Binion bought the property at 128 Fremont Street in Las Vegas and changed Western
hospitality, bringing Texas-style good food, good odds, and friendly service — a more democratic
gambling environment than the exclusive clubs elsewhere. (His name became attached to what would
grow into a landmark of American poker.)

### Herbert Noble

Binion's chief rival in the Dallas scene, whose feud with Binion became legendary and violent —
involving multiple assassination attempts and bombings. Noble's operations spanned numbers running,
bookmaking, and casino games; he died in a car bombing in 1951.

### The Maceo brothers

Rose and Sam Maceo transformed **Galveston** into the "**Las Vegas of the South**" in the
1920s–1940s. Their operations included the famous **Balinese Room**, a casino built on a pier over
the Gulf of Mexico; numbers running throughout Southeast Texas; protection from local law
enforcement through political corruption; and entertainment venues that drew celebrities and
wealthy gamblers from across the region.

### The oil barons

Texas oil millionaires like H.L. Hunt and Clint Murchison were legendary high-stakes gamblers —
participating in million-dollar poker games and betting enormous sums on horse races and sporting
events, in a culture where business deals and gambling debts often intermingled.

## The road to the Texas Lottery

By the 1980s, budget pressures and the visible success of lotteries in other states shifted public
opinion toward viewing lotteries as an acceptable form of entertainment and revenue. In **1991**,
Texas voters approved a **constitutional amendment** allowing a state lottery — a dramatic reversal
of longstanding policy. The **Texas Lottery began operations in 1992**; first-day sales for the
"Lone Star Millions" scratch game set a record, and the lottery quickly became one of the most
successful in the nation, with proceeds directed to public education, which helped legitimize it.

The success of the regulated game, combined with stronger enforcement, made it hard for illegal
operations to compete, and the traditional numbers game faded.

## The modern Texas landscape

**Legal today:** the Texas Lottery (including scratch-offs and multi-state games), charitable
bingo, church and nonprofit raffles, horse and dog racing with pari-mutuel betting, and daily
fantasy sports in limited forms.

**Still prohibited:** casino gambling, slot machines outside certain tribal lands, sports betting
(beyond limited daily fantasy), and most forms of online gambling.

The evolution — from frontier vice to a major source of education funding — mirrors broader American
attitudes toward risk, entertainment, and government revenue. What was once treated as morally
questionable became a state institution.
