# California Gambling History: From Gold Rush to Hollywood Glitz

*Neutral history and cultural reference.*

California's gambling history runs from the mud and gold dust of the 1849 mining camps to the
floating casinos and mob-run clubs of the Hollywood era, and finally to the voter-approved state
lottery of 1985. It is a story of larger-than-life characters, jurisdiction-shopping innovation,
and a state whose constitution long prohibited gambling even as demand for it never stopped.

## The Gold Rush era (1848–1860s): where dreams and dice collided

The gold fields were about risk-taking in every form. The Englishman J.D. Borthwick, who came to
California in 1851 seeking his fortune, documented the gambling everywhere in his lithographs and
narrative. Boomtowns from Sacramento to San Francisco became legendary gambling centers where miners
bet gold dust on:

- **Faro** — the most popular card game of the era
- **Monte** — a Spanish card game that dominated early California
- Poker and other card games
- Dice games and roulette wheels

Gambling cut across all social classes: wealthy merchants, desperate miners, and professional
gamblers gathered at the same tables, creating a briefly democratic atmosphere where fortunes could
be made or lost in a single hand.

## The Hollywood era (1920s–1950s): glamour meets the underworld

### Anthony Cornero, "the Admiral"

Anthony Cornero Stralla (1899–1955), known as "the Admiral" and "Tony the Hat," was a bootlegger and
gambling entrepreneur in Southern California from the 1920s through the 1950s. His signature
innovation was **jurisdiction-shopping by sea**: operating gambling ships beyond the three-mile
limit, outside U.S. jurisdiction. His famous gambling ship *Rex*, anchored off Santa Monica, was a
luxurious floating casino that drew Hollywood stars and pioneered the concept of "international
waters" gambling.

### Mickey Cohen and the Sunset Strip

Meyer Harris "Mickey" Cohen moved from Brooklyn to Los Angeles as a boy and rose through the city's
illegal-gambling world — eventually a Hollywood mob figure whose empire included high-stakes poker
games for movie executives and stars, luxury illegal casinos known as "**rug-joints**," protection
rackets, and bookmaking across Los Angeles. In a rich, hedonistic Hollywood where Golden Age movie
moguls gambled daily, the rug-joints became legendary meeting places for the elite. The **Sunset
Strip** became synonymous with high-stakes gambling — exclusive clubs behind restaurant facades,
regular raids, and territorial disputes among competing organizations, in a culture where gambling
debts and Hollywood careers intertwined. A famous 1945 casino raid, led by Cohen (once an enforcer
for Benjamin "Bugsy" Siegel's operations), demonstrated the territorial nature of illegal Hollywood
gambling.

## Numbers running in California

Less documented than East Coast cities, California's urban areas — particularly Los Angeles,
Oakland, and San Francisco — had their own **numbers game** operations, serving working-class
communities including the African-American population that migrated west during and after World War
II. As elsewhere, the game provided employment in underserved neighborhoods, informal credit for
residents excluded from banks, social gathering points, and economic circulation within communities
with limited access to mainstream finance.

## The path to legalization

California's constitution historically prohibited most gambling, but enforcement was selective and
often shaped by political connections — the tension between legal prohibition and popular demand
created openings for organized operations. The turning point came with **Proposition 37, the
California State Lottery Act of 1984**; the **California State Lottery began in October 1985**. It
marked a dramatic shift: recognition of gambling's revenue potential for public education,
acknowledgment that prohibition had failed to eliminate gambling, and a transition from underground
operations to regulated gaming.

## Cultural legacy

California's gambling story features characters who became American folklore — the gold-rush
gamblers who risked everything on the turn of a card, Cornero's floating casinos that challenged
federal jurisdiction, Cohen's fusion of gambling with Hollywood glamour, and the community numbers
runners who served as informal bankers. California's evolution influenced national trends: the
concept of **destination gambling** (from gold-rush towns to Las Vegas), the **integration of
gambling with entertainment** (the Hollywood model), the use of technology and jurisdiction-shopping
(the gambling ships), and community-based informal gambling networks.
