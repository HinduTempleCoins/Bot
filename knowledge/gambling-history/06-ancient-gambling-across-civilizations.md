# Ancient Gambling Across Civilizations: From Sumerian Dice to Chinese Lotteries

*Neutral history and cultural reference. Overlaps the ancient-history and mystery-school shelves by
design.*

Gambling appears, independently, in nearly every major ancient civilization — evidence that it
addresses something durable in human nature: the desire for community, shared risk, mathematical
engagement, and collective hope. This article surveys that ancient record, from Mesopotamian dice to
Chinese state lotteries, and notes a recurring pattern: gambling repeatedly drove advances in
mathematics, law, and religion, and it consistently served social functions beyond entertainment.

## Mesopotamia: the cradle of games of chance

### Sumer (c. 3500–1900 BC)

The **Royal Game of Ur** — a two-player race board game found among personal items in the Royal
Graves of Ur (deposited c. 2600 BC) — is the oldest playable board game in the world, roughly 4,600
years old. Boards have been found from humble homes to royal palaces, showing that gambling
transcended social boundaries in the world's first urban civilization. Remarkably, the game's rules
were written on a cuneiform tablet by a Babylonian astronomer in **177 BC** — meaning it stayed
popular for over 2,000 years. The Sumerians are credited with early games like "20 squares" and "58
holes," using dice and numbered squares that required mathematical literacy, making gaming an
educational tool.

### Babylon (1894–539 BC)

Some of the earliest known dice date to the late third millennium BC, from the Indus Valley and
Mesopotamia. Babylonian astronomers — who created the zodiac and advanced mathematics — applied
their numerical expertise to gambling probability. Evidence suggests professional gambling classes
operated in Babylonian cities, and Babylonian law codes addressed gambling debts and disputes,
creating some of the world's first gambling regulations. While the **Code of Hammurabi** did not
explicitly address gambling, its principles of fairness, contract enforcement, and property rights
shaped how gaming disputes were resolved.

### Elam (3200–539 BC)

The often-forgotten Elamite civilization (centered on Susa) produced some of the world's earliest
**standardized dice**, using precise measurements that influenced later Mesopotamian gaming. Elamite
tombs held luxury gaming sets of precious stones and metals, and Elamite priests used dice and boards
as **oracle gaming** — communicating with deities and predicting futures — establishing gambling as
legitimate religious practice. Elam's position between Mesopotamia and the Iranian plateau made it a
crucial transmission point.

The Mesopotamian region as a whole functioned as a laboratory for gaming innovation, spreading
traditions through trade routes, military campaigns, and religious exchange, and contributing
standardized equipment, the base-60 mathematical system, and early legal frameworks.

## Ancient Egypt: the dawn of dice (c. 3000 BC)

The first historical evidence of dice games dates to the Egyptians around 3000 BC (societies likely
had them earlier). Ivory dice and carved gaming boards have been found in tombs dating back to 2000
BC. Egyptian gambling was not limited to commoners — pharaohs and nobility were buried with elaborate
gaming sets, suggesting gambling was thought worthy of the afterlife. Different classes used different
materials (ivory for elite, bone or wood for common people) but played similar games. Egyptian gaming
artifacts show a sophisticated understanding of probability that paralleled their innovations in
architecture and astronomy.

## Ancient China: the lottery pioneers (c. 2300 BC)

The earliest evidence of gambling is dated around 2300 BC in ancient China, where tiles used for
chance games and lottery-type games have been unearthed. Chinese lotteries **funded significant state
projects**, establishing a model of government-sponsored gambling for public benefit that continues
today — some historians believe early Chinese lotteries helped fund major construction projects,
potentially including early fortifications that would become part of the Great Wall. The **I Ching**
(Book of Changes) connected divination to number selection through a system of 64 hexagrams generated
by coin tosses — a synthesis of philosophy, mathematics, and chance. Traditional Chinese games —
imperial cricket fighting, **Pai Gow** (domino games), early tile games that became mahjong, and
organized animal contests — influenced gaming culture throughout Asia.

## Ancient India: the Vedic tradition (c. 1700 BC)

In the **Rig Veda** (c. 1700 BC), a gambler pleads with the dice to spare him — one of the earliest
recorded pieces of gambling literature, showing that both the appeal and the dangers of gambling
were understood thousands of years ago. Throughout the medieval period, **Satta** ("numbers")
persisted as a direct predecessor to modern numbers games and lotteries. Horse racing also appears in
Vedic texts, establishing one of the world's oldest traditions of organized betting on competitive
events.

## Ancient Greece: games of the gods

In Greek mythology, **Hades, Poseidon, and Zeus cast lots** to divide the universe — a mythological
foundation that gave gambling divine sanction. The ancient **Olympic Games** included extensive
betting by spectators, establishing the template for sports betting. Greek philosophers developed
sophisticated thinking about chance, fate, and probability: Stoic acceptance of outcomes as cosmic
order, mathematical analysis of odds, and ethical debate about gambling's moral implications that
influenced later Western thought.

## Ancient Rome: imperial gaming (753 BC–476 AD)

Romans favored dice games (**Tali** and **Tesserae**), the wealthy playing on jewel-encrusted marble
boards. Gambling with dice was illegal — **except during the Saturnalia festival** — an early parallel
to modern seasonal/regulated gambling and to sophisticated thinking about social control and release.
Roman analysis of gambling was psychologically astute, recognizing the adrenaline of risk and its
addictive potential — an early understanding of gambling as both entertainment and social problem.
The **casting of lots** is the origin of the modern term "lottery," and it is referenced in both the
Old and New Testaments — most famously when Roman soldiers cast lots for Jesus' clothes at the
crucifixion. Gambling in Rome crossed all classes, practiced among slaves, masters, and for a time
even emperors.

## Ancient Persia: imperial gaming culture (550–331 BC)

**Nard** (originally Nardashir), the ancestor of modern **backgammon**, originated with the Persians;
the earliest source to mention it by name is the **Babylonian Talmud** — evidence of cultural
exchange between Persian and Jewish communities. Zoroastrianism, with its cosmic battle between good
(Ahura Mazda) and evil (Angra Mainyu), provided a theological framework for understanding chance, and
its ethics created cultural pressure for honest dealing. Persian royalty considered gaming essential
to noble education and used games for diplomacy, military strategy training, and cultural exchange
across the empire's tolerated diversity.

## Ancient Jewish tradition: Talmudic tensions

Among the ancient Israelites there is no early mention of games of chance; dice play (*kubya*, Greek
*kubeia*) was adopted later, in the Second Temple period, as foreign customs spread. The main Talmudic
discussion appears in **Sanhedrin (24b–25a)**: the Mishnah disqualifies a dice player (*mesacheik
bekubiya*) from serving as a witness. There is no explicit Jewish prohibition on gambling, but the
rabbis viewed it unfavorably, and the Gemara's concept of **asmachta** — an agreement based on
speculation — reflects sophisticated rabbinic analysis of gambling psychology and contract law.
Tensions persisted for centuries: in 1628 the rabbis of Venice issued a six-year decree
excommunicating any Jew who gambled — evidence both of gambling's prevalence in Jewish communities and
the ongoing religious tension around it. Babylonian Jews, active in international trade, adopted
Persian Nard and used gambling as business entertainment while developing ways to accommodate it
within religious frameworks.

## Cross-cultural exchange

The **Silk Road** and Mediterranean trade networks spread gaming culture: games traveled along trade
routes and fused into hybrid traditions, professional gamblers spread techniques, and gambling became
part of international trade and diplomacy. Roman gambling adapted Greek traditions; Egyptian
mathematical and gaming innovations influenced both; Arabian mathematicians contributed crucial
advances in **algebra and probability** and numerical systems that shaped later gambling analysis;
and pre-Islamic Arabian cultures had elaborate betting on camel racing, poetry contests, and tribal
games of chance along the trade routes.

## What the ancient record teaches

The remarkable consistency of gambling across ancient civilizations — Egyptian dice, Chinese
lotteries, Indian Vedic horse racing, Roman casting of lots, Mesopotamian board games — shows that
games of chance are one of humanity's oldest and most universal traditions. Each civilization
contributed something enduring: Egyptian mathematical precision, Chinese government-sponsored
lotteries for public benefit, Indian community integration and religious accommodation, Greek
athletic betting and philosophical analysis, Roman legal frameworks and cross-class participation,
Arabian mathematical probability, Persian courtly refinement, and the Jewish tradition's rigorous
legal-ethical analysis. Understood this way, modern lotteries and lottery pools are less an
invention than the **latest chapter** in a 5,000-year-old cultural practice that has always mixed
mathematics, community, and shared hope.
