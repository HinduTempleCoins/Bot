# Modern Lottery Integrity: The 2023 Bulk-Buy Jackpot, Couriers, and the "Rigged" Question

*Neutral history and reference. Reports publicly documented events and allegations as reported;
where matters were contested or under investigation, that is noted. Not legal advice or a verdict.*

The traditional illegal numbers game faded with the rise of legal state lotteries, but the modern
era shows that manipulation and integrity questions did not disappear — they moved upmarket, into
sophisticated schemes and third-party technology that blur the line between what is illegal and what
is merely controversial. The Texas Lottery has been the focus of several of the most-discussed
recent episodes.

## The 2023 bulk-buy jackpot

In 2023, a group of investors — reportedly including London-based traders — set out to purchase
**nearly every possible number combination** for a **Lotto Texas** jackpot. Using sophisticated
methods (including acquiring specialized lottery terminals) and, per reporting, with some level of
cooperation from lottery officials, they spent about **$25 million** to lock up the roughly
**$95 million** prize. Because a draw-game like Lotto Texas has a fixed, knowable number of
combinations, buying them all guarantees holding the winning ticket — the strategy's only real risks
are a split jackpot and the logistics of printing millions of tickets in time.

The scheme sparked public outrage and investigations by state officials, some of whom called it
"the biggest theft" in Texas lottery history. The core controversy: whether it was legal for a
syndicate to effectively corner a public lottery, and whether officials should have enabled the bulk
printing. It is best understood not as a rigged **draw** (the numbers were drawn fairly) but as a
question of whether the **rules and infrastructure** allowed a well-capitalized group to convert a
game of chance into a near-certain purchase.

## Third-party couriers: the Jackpocket / DraftKings question

Around the same time, an **$83.5 million** jackpot was won through a **third-party lottery-courier
app, Jackpocket**, which purchased tickets on behalf of a user. Jackpocket was owned by **DraftKings**,
which reporting noted also had a relationship with the store that printed the ticket — raising
questions about potential conflicts of interest. Courier apps occupy a genuinely gray zone: they let
customers order tickets remotely while the app buys physical tickets at a licensed retailer. The
integrity questions concern transparency, the concentration of the retail/courier/operator
relationship in fewer hands, and whether such arrangements are consistent with lottery rules.

## The *Rigged* documentary (2025)

A 2025 documentary, ***Rigged: How the Lottery is Playing Texans***, alleged a history of
mismanagement and scandal within the Texas Lottery Commission, including claims about corrupt
vendors and officials who cooperated with schemes. The film alleged the agency had authorized selling
tickets to out-of-state players and approved a payout to an overseas winner. These are the
documentary's allegations; they belong in the record as claims that fueled the public and political
scrutiny of the lottery's operations, not as adjudicated findings.

## Why integrity is the through-line

Read alongside the numbers-game history, these episodes make a consistent point about lotteries in
every era:

- **Legitimacy depends on an outcome no participant can control or corner.** The old numbers bankers
  drew their winning number from public data precisely so it couldn't be rigged. Modern draw games
  use audited, random draws for the same reason. The bulk-buy episode shows the failure mode isn't
  a rigged draw — it's rules and infrastructure that let a syndicate buy the whole board.
- **New intermediaries create new integrity questions.** Courier apps and vendor relationships add
  convenience and also add places for conflicts of interest to hide. Transparency is the mitigant.
- **"Rigged" is often about governance, not the RNG.** Public trust in a lottery rests less on the
  randomness of the balls than on the honesty of the institution running it — vendor selection,
  payout approvals, and who is allowed to play and how.

For a platform that publishes gambling **education**, the lesson is to teach players what actually
protects them (auditable draws, published odds, disclosed rules) and to be candid that the
controversies are real — while never itself running a book or holding a stake.
