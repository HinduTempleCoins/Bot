# Sais & Delphi — character briefs for figures without surviving portraits

For Hathor Studio renders. Each figure has: who they were (from sources actually read), era, a period-accurate
VISUAL brief with a source per claim, and a render prompt (`{P}` = the people's ethnicity slot).
Reference crops for these briefs are in the `oracles` remake set (`oracles_scenes_6.jsonl`); keys are cited inline.

Status markers: **[attested]** = stated by a primary text or museum/catalogue record; **[secondary]** = stated by a
modern encyclopedia/summary; **[uncertain]** = disputed, uncited, or a modern theory; **[inference]** = our
reconstruction from period evidence, not a statement about this individual.

---

## 1. Peseshet — "Overseer of female physicians" (Old Kingdom)

**Who.** Peseshet (*psšt*, also spelled Pesshet) appears with Kanefer (probably her husband) on a false door in the
chapel of Giza mastaba **G 8942**, which belonged to Akhethetep. Her titles on the door: "overseer of female
physicians, royal acquaintance, overseer of ka-priests of the king's mother" **[attested]**
(Digital Giza, *Peseshet (in G 8942)*, citing Selim Hassan, *Excavations at Gîza 1: 1929–1930*, 1932, and
Baud, *BIFAO* 95, 1995 — http://giza.fas.harvard.edu/ancientpeople/346/full/).

- **Era:** 4th Dynasty; a 5th Dynasty date is also possible **[secondary]** (Wikipedia, *Peseshet*).
- **Title reading** — *imy-r swnwt*, "(lady) overseer of the (lady) physicians" **[uncertain in detail]**. The
  feminine *swnwt* is the basis of the "female physicians" reading. Wikipedia notes it is **not certain she practised
  medicine herself**; the title may be purely administrative (overseeing women physicians).
- **Son:** Digital Giza treats her as Akhethetep's mother; Wikipedia says the mother–son link is **not confirmed by an
  inscription** **[uncertain]**.
- **Sais midwife school:** the popular claim that she "graduated midwives at a medical school in Sais" is flagged
  *citation needed* on Wikipedia **[uncertain — do not present as fact]**.
- Not the same as "Merit-Ptah", the supposed "first woman physician". Wikipedia says that figure is probably
  fictional, from a 1938 book that borrowed Peseshet's details.

**Visual brief** (no portrait survives, so this is an **[inference]** from Old Kingdom elite women of her rank):
- **Dress:** ankle-length white linen sheath dress (*kalasiris*) with one or two shoulder straps. The upper edge
  sits above or below the breast (Wikipedia, *Clothing in ancient Egypt*). Model: Nofret's white sheath mantle
  (`oldkingdom_rahotep_nofret`, Dynasty 4, Meidum; Egyptian Museum Cairo).
- **Wig/hair:** heavy, shoulder-length dark wig with natural hair visible at the brow, as on Nofret. Wikipedia,
  *Nofret*: "a dark thick wig" with "dark straight hair" beneath.
- **Jewellery:** broad collar (*wesekh*) (Clothing in ancient Egypt) and a floral diadem "similar to actual diadems
  made of gold with inlays found at Giza" (Wikipedia, *Nofret*). Note that Nofret also held the title "King's
  Acquaintance", the same court rank as Peseshet.
- **Props [inference]:** a scribe's palette or papyrus roll for the administrator, plus small stone ointment jars
  and linen bandages for the medical side. Old Kingdom treatment of hands and feet is shown in the Ankhmahor tomb
  relief at Saqqara, Dynasty 6 (`medicine_ankhmahor_treatment`), which is slightly later than Peseshet.
- **Setting:** a mastaba chapel with a false door, or a plain limestone hall.

**Render prompt:**
> Photorealistic full-body portrait of Peseshet, an Old Kingdom Egyptian noblewoman of {P} appearance, overseer of
> the women physicians, c. 2500 BCE. She wears an ankle-length white linen sheath dress with two shoulder straps, a
> broad beaded collar in blue, green and red faience, a heavy shoulder-length black wig with her natural hairline
> showing beneath, and a gold diadem inlaid with floral rosettes. She holds a papyrus roll in one hand. Beside her
> on a low table are alabaster ointment jars and folded linen bandages. She stands calm and authoritative, barefoot.
> Plain limestone temple hall with a carved false door behind her. Soft daylight, museum-accurate detail.

## 2. Sonchis of Sais — the priest who told Solon of Atlantis

**Who.**
- **Plutarch, *Life of Solon* 26.1** (Perrin tr., LacusCurtius): Solon "also spent some time in studies with
  Psenophis of Heliopolis and Sonchis of Saïs, who were very learned priests. From these, as Plato says, he heard
  the story of the lost Atlantis" **[attested — in Plutarch, c. 100 CE]**.
- **Plato, *Timaeus* 21e–22b** (Perseus): Sais was founded by a goddess called Neith in Egyptian, "and in Greek, as
  they assert, Athena". The Saites claim kinship with the Athenians. The priest who answers Solon is **unnamed**:
  "one of the priests, a prodigiously old man", who says "O Solon, Solon, you Greeks are always children"
  **[attested]**.
- **Identification [uncertain]:** Plato never names the priest. The name Sonchis comes only from Plutarch, some
  five centuries later. "Sonchis = the Timaeus priest" is Plutarch's attribution, not Plato's.
- **Era:** Solon's Egyptian journey falls after his archonship (594/3 BCE). That puts it in the Saite 26th Dynasty,
  when Sais was Egypt's capital (664–525 BCE; Wikipedia, *Neith*), and Plutarch places it under Amasis. Plato
  dates the Atlantis tale itself 9,000 years earlier.

**Visual brief:**
- **Head and body:** shaved. Herodotus 2.37 (Macaulay tr., Gutenberg #2131): "The priests shave themselves all over
  their body every other day" **[attested]**.
- **Dress:** "garments of linen only and sandals of papyrus" (Herodotus 2.37). A high-ranking priest wears a
  leopard skin over the white linen (Wikipedia, *Clothing in ancient Egypt*).
  - Period model: the Late Period bronze of a shaven-headed priest in a leopard cloak, the paw hanging at the
    front, with a long kilt (Met 26.7.1415; `lateperiod_priest_leopard`).
  - A leopard-skinned priest also appears in Ernest Board's modern painting (`medicine_imhotep_invocation`). That
    painting is a modern imagining, not evidence.
- **Age:** "a prodigiously old man" (Timaeus 22b), if Sonchis is the Timaeus priest.
- **Props [inference]:** papyrus roll(s) of temple annals (the Timaeus priest cites written records kept in the
  temple). Temple of Neith at Sais: her emblem of two crossed bows over a shield, which was also the city's sign
  (Wikipedia, *Neith*). The Feast of Lamps, where saucers of salt and oil burned all night (Herodotus 2.62),
  could be used for a night variant.

**Render prompt:**
> Photorealistic full-body portrait of Sonchis, a very old Egyptian priest of Neith at Sais, of {P} appearance,
> c. 570 BCE. His head and face are cleanly shaven and his body is lean and aged. He wears a long pleated white
> linen kilt and robe with a spotted leopard skin draped over one shoulder, the paw hanging at his chest, and
> papyrus sandals. He holds an unrolled papyrus of temple annals. Behind him is a plain sandstone temple wall with
> the crossed-bows-and-shield emblem of Neith carved in relief. Warm lamp light, dignified and patient expression.

**Scene variant — Solon with the priests** (no public-domain artwork of this was found on Commons; see report):
> Photorealistic scene in the temple of Neith at Sais, c. 570 BCE: an elderly {P} Egyptian priest with a shaved head
> in white linen and leopard skin shows temple papyri to Solon of Athens, a bearded {P} Greek statesman in a
> wool himation. Other shaven-headed priests in white linen stand near, in a plain columned hall.

## 3. Udjahorresnet — chief physician, priest of Neith (portrait EXISTS)

**Who.** Official of the 26th–27th Dynasties **[attested / secondary]** (Wikipedia, *Udjahorresnet*; livius.org,
*Wedjahor-Resne*; Renouf translation on attalus.org):
- He was commander of the royal navy under Amasis II and Psamtik III.
- After Cambyses conquered Egypt (Pelusium, 525 BCE), he became chief physician. He composed Cambyses' pharaonic
  titulary and served Darius I.
- Darius sent him back from Elam to Egypt. Renouf's translation: "His Majesty... gave orders that I should come to
  Egypt... to re-establish the school of the Hierogrammatists and (restore) what had fallen in ruin."
- Modern translators render the institution as the **House of Life (per-ankh)** at Sais. A secondary summary of
  Lichtheim's *AEL* III says it was restored because Darius "knew the virtue of this art to revive all that are
  sick", which is the medical dimension **[secondary — Lichtheim not read directly]**.
- A 2020 scholarly article (Lopez, *JAEI* 26) links the reform to Darius' ankle injury, which the Greek physician
  Democedes healed **[secondary — scholarly hypothesis]**.
- Opening titles (Renouf): "The one honored by Neith-the-Great, the mother of god... prince, count, royal
  seal-bearer, sole companion... commander of the royal navy".

**The portrait — naophorous statue, Vatican (Museo Gregoriano Egizio, label no. 22690).** Described from the
Commons photographs (`sais_udjahorresnet_statue`, Gary Todd, PD; plus a gallery view by Dawid Skalec, CC BY-SA
3.0) and livius.org:
- **Material and size:** dark green-grey stone (the museum label reads "Basalto verde"); about 70 cm high.
- **History:** set up in the temple of Neith at Sais around Darius' year 3. Hadrian later brought it to his villa
  at Tivoli.
- **Condition:** as currently displayed the statue is headless **[observed in the photos]**.
- **Pose and naos:** he stands holding before him a naos (shrine) containing a figure of Osiris.
- **Robe:** a long, plain wrap-around robe reaching to the ankles, covered in columns of inscription.
- **Bracelets:** visible on the wrist. The text says his masters "bestowed upon me decorations of gold" (Renouf).
- **Feet:** bare, on a plinth.
- The long enveloping robe is typical of Late Period/Persian-era elite statues **[inference]**.

**Render prompt (head reconstructed [inference] — shaved head of a Late Period priest, per Herodotus 2.37):**
> Photorealistic full-body portrait of Udjahorresnet, Egyptian chief physician and priest of Neith at Sais, of {P}
> appearance, c. 519 BCE, middle-aged. His head is shaved. He wears a long ankle-length wrap-around white linen robe
> tied high under the arms, gold bracelets of honour on his wrists, and papyrus sandals. He holds before him a
> small dark-stone naos shrine with a figure of Osiris inside. Plain temple hall at Sais with papyrus rolls on
> shelves (the House of Life). Composed, scholarly bearing.

## 4. The Pythia — priestess of Apollo at Delphi

**Who / practice** (Wikipedia, *Pythia*, summarising Plutarch, Diodorus, Herodotus, Euripides and Pausanias)
**[secondary]**:
- The oracle was active from at least the 8th century BCE until about 390 CE.
- Early Pythiai were young virgins. After an assault on one of them, Diodorus says "an elderly woman of fifty"
  prophesied "dressed in the costume of a virgin".
- Pythiai were chosen from Delphian women, of any class.
- On the 7th day of the month she bathed in the **Castalian spring** and drank from Kassotis. Petitioners
  sacrificed a goat after a water-sprinkle omen.
- She sat on the **tripod** in the adyton, holding **laurel leaves and a dish of Kassotis water**, near the
  **omphalos**.
- **Vapours [uncertain]:** Plutarch attributes her power to vapours. De Boer's modern ethylene hypothesis is a
  theory, not settled.

**Visual brief:**
- **Earliest image — Attic red-figure kylix by the Kodros Painter, c. 440–430 BCE, Berlin F2538**
  (`delphi_aegeus_themis_kylix`) **[attested object; the seated woman is labelled Themis, standing in for the
  Pythia]**:
  - She wears a long chiton with a himation drawn over her head as a veil, and a fillet in her curly hair.
  - She is seated on a tall tripod, holding a shallow **phiale** in one hand and a **laurel sprig** in the other.
  - A Doric column marks the temple, and King Aegeus stands before her.
- **Later painting:** John Collier, *Priestess of Delphi* (1891; `delphi_collier_priestess`) shows her on a
  lion-footed tripod over a fissure with vapour, holding laurel and bowl. It is a 19th-century imagining; the red
  mantle and embroidered gown are Collier's choice **[not evidence]**.
- **Omphalos and tripod together:** Paestan krater by Python, c. 330 BCE, BM 1917.12-10.1 (`delphi_orestes_krater`).

**Render prompt:**
> Photorealistic full-body portrait of the Pythia of Delphi, a {P} woman in the white linen chiton of a maiden, with
> a himation drawn over her head as a veil and a wool fillet in her hair, 5th century BCE. She sits upright on a tall
> bronze tripod in the dim stone adyton of the Temple of Apollo, holding a shallow gold phiale of spring water in one
> hand and a sprig of laurel in the other. Beside her is the carved omphalos stone, and a faint haze rises from the
> rock floor. Plain ashlar walls. Solemn, inward-looking expression.

## 5. A priestess of Neith at Sais

**Who (type, not an individual):**
- Old Kingdom elite women commonly held the title *ḥmt-nṯr* ("priestess", literally "servant of the god") of Hathor
  or Neith. The title is common in the Old and Middle Kingdoms but falls out of use by the New Kingdom
  **[secondary]** (web summaries of *Titles in Ancient Egypt* / Diotíma, *Women and Religion in Ancient Egypt*).
- Example: Queen Hetepheres II held "Priestess of Neith" (*ḥmt-nṯr Nt*) **[secondary]**.
- Queens served as priestesses of Neith and Hathor (Diotíma).
- **Late Period (Saite) priestesses of Neith at Sais** — no specific source read **[uncertain]**; the Old Kingdom
  type below is the best-attested version.

**The goddess she serves** (Wikipedia, *Neith*):
- Neith wears the Red Crown of Lower Egypt and carries bow and arrows.
- Her emblem is two crossed bows over a shield, also the city sign of Sais.
- She is a weaving goddess.
- Her main cult centre is Sais, capital of the 26th Dynasty.
- The Sais inscription reads "I am all that has been, is, and will be. No mortal has ever lifted my veil" (Plutarch,
  *De Iside* 9; Proclus).
- References:
  - Tomb of Nefertari figure (`sais_neith_nefertari`, Museo Egizio archive): tripartite wig, emblem on the head,
    broad collar, bead-net sheath.
  - Late Period seated bronze with the Red Crown (`sais_neith_seated_bronze`, LACMA 50.14.1).

**Neith ↔ Athena ↔ Tanit — what is ancient and what is theory:**
- **Neith = Athena: ancient Greek claim [attested].** Plato, *Timaeus* 21e: Sais' founding goddess is "Neith... and
  in Greek, as they assert, Athena", and the Saites call themselves kin of the Athenians. Herodotus also calls the
  goddess of Sais "Athene" (2.59, 2.62).
- **Athena from Libya: ancient Greek claim [attested].** Herodotus 4.189 says Athena's garments and aegis were
  borrowed from Libyan women. The epithet Tritogeneia is tied to Lake Tritonis (secondary summary).
- **Tanit = Neith = Athena: no ancient text read makes this triple identification [uncertain / modern theory].**
  Wikipedia's *Tanit* states "whom the Egyptians identify as Neith and the Greeks identify as Athena" but cites no
  ancient source for the Neith part. Romans are attested identifying Tanit with Juno Caelestis.
- **Neith/Tanit as the pre-Athena goddess of Athens:** this is the operator's framework position. Its nearest
  ancient support is the Timaeus kinship claim. It is not a direct ancient statement.

**Visual brief** (Old Kingdom–Saite priestess **[inference]** built from attested items):
- **Dress:** white linen sheath dress (*kalasiris*) (Clothing in ancient Egypt).
- **Wig and collar:** heavy tripartite wig and broad collar, as worn by Neith herself in the Nefertari tomb.
- **Instruments:** priestesses carried the **sistrum** and **menat** necklace (Diotíma — attested from the Middle
  Kingdom onward; for an Old Kingdom setting, treat these as **[inference]**).
- **Props:** a small shield-and-crossed-bows emblem standard or amulet of Neith, and a spindle or linen on a loom
  (Neith as weaver).
- **Night-festival variant:** saucer lamps of salt and oil (Herodotus 2.62).

**Render prompt:**
> Photorealistic full-body portrait of a priestess of Neith at Sais, a {P} woman, c. 600 BCE. She wears an
> ankle-length white linen sheath dress, a broad collar of faience beads, and a heavy black tripartite wig bound with
> a red fillet. She holds a bronze sistrum in one hand, and a menat necklace hangs from the other. Behind her, a
> small gilded emblem of two crossed bows over a shield stands on a pole. There is an upright loom with white linen
> at one side. Plain sandstone temple sanctuary lit by rows of small oil saucer lamps. Serene, watchful expression.
